# CARSON — Next Steps

Supply chain, sneaker production, and the road to a first release.

Written from what is known: a thirty-year Agra manufacturer with 35,000
pairs/month capacity, own leather sourcing through Kolkata and Chennai, an
Italian seasonal design partner, SA-8000 certification, and an export history
to Europe, the US, the Gulf and Southeast Asia.

Last updated: 2026-08-25

> **The structural advantage, stated once so it informs everything below:**
> you already have the hardest and most expensive part of a footwear brand.
> A normal DTC launch spends 12–18 months and most of its capital finding a
> factory, qualifying it, and getting samples that do not fall apart. You skip
> that entirely. Your risk is not *can we make it* — it is *can we sell it*.
> Every decision below should protect cash and speed while you find that out.

## 2. Supply chain

### 2.1 What you already control

| Layer | Status | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Leather sourcing | Own network, Kolkata + Chennai | **Low** — decades of relationships |
| Cutting, closing, lasting, finishing | In-house | **Low** |
| Design | Italian partner, seasonal | **Medium** — dependent on one relationship |
| Compliance | SA-8000, audited | **Low** — renew on schedule |
| Capacity | 35,000 pairs/month | **None** — vastly more than a DTC launch needs |

You are vertically integrated from hide to finished shoe. That is rare and it is
the entire commercial argument.

### 2.2 What you do NOT yet control — the real gaps

These are the pieces a B2B exporter has never needed and a consumer brand cannot
launch without.

| Gap | Why it bites | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Retail packaging** | Export cartons are not a unboxing. At £265 the box IS the product for the first thirty seconds. | Source a rigid two-piece box, tissue, dust bags, a care card. Budget ₹250–500/unit. |
| **DTC-grade QC** | Trade buyers accept an AQL with defects. A single customer receiving one bad pair writes a review that costs more than the pair. | **100% inspection** on every DTC pair. At launch volumes this is trivial and non-negotiable. |
| **Single-pair pick/pack/ship** | Your logistics move pallets, not parcels. | Set up a small dispatch bench. DHL/FedEx account for international. |
| **Size curve for DTC** | Wholesale orders arrive with a curve. DTC demand is unpredictable and returns are size-driven. | Hold depth in UK 8–10 (≈60% of demand); make 6, 7, 11, 12 to order. |
| **Returns handling** | You have never taken a shoe back from a consumer. | Write the policy, define who inspects, decide what happens to returned stock. |
| **Duties / landed cost** | The site says "duties included". That is a promise with a cost. | Model DDP landed cost per market before setting price. |

### 2.3 Recommended launch model — make to order, small buffer

Do **not** build inventory before you have demand data.

- Hold a **buffer of 30–50 pairs** across the core size band per style.
- Everything else made to order against your own line, 10–14 day turnaround.
- Advertise the lead time honestly: *"Made to order in Agra. Ships in 14 days."*
  At this price, a wait reads as craft, not as failure — but only if you say it
  up front rather than after they have paid.

This costs you almost nothing in a factory you already run, and it means a
failed launch loses you hundreds of pounds instead of tens of thousands.

## 3. Sneaker production

Handled separately because it is the hardest thing in the plan and the founder
has named it as the hero product.

### 3.1 The honest problem

Your thirty years are in **formal and smart leather footwear** — cemented and
welted dress construction, oxfords, derbies, boots. The showroom photography
confirms it. That expertise transfers to loafers and boots almost completely.

**It does not transfer cleanly to sneakers.** A premium leather sneaker is a
different product:

| | Dress shoe | Leather sneaker |
|---|---|---|
| Construction | Cemented / welted, leather sole | Vulcanised or cupsole, rubber |
| Last | Narrow, structured | Wider, higher toe spring, volume |
| Tolerance | Forgiving — a formal shoe is expected to break in | Unforgiving — judged on comfort out of the box |
| Sole sourcing | In-house or local | **Specialist cupsole supplier** |
| Benchmark | Your own export standard | Common Projects, Axel Arigato — customers compare directly |

### 3.2 Recommendation: sneaker third, not first

**Lead the launch with loafers and boots.** Add the sneaker as the third
silhouette once the brand has revenue and a reputation.

Reasons, in order of weight:

1. **You are already excellent at the other two.** Launching where you are best
   means your first hundred customers get your best work. First reviews are
   permanent.
2. **Sneakers are the most crowded premium category in footwear**, with the most
   entrenched incumbents and the most educated buyers.
3. **A sneaker needs a new last, a new sole supplier and a wear-test cycle** —
   three to six months of development before it is worth selling.
4. **Loafers and boots carry the story better.** "Thirty years on the bench"
   sounds true of a welted boot. It sounds borrowed on a sneaker.

*This is a recommendation, not a blocker. If you want the sneaker first, the
development plan below stands — it just needs to start now.*

### 3.3 Sneaker development plan (if pursued)

**Phase 1 — Last and sole (6–8 weeks)**
- Commission or buy a sneaker last. Do not adapt a dress last; the volume and
  toe spring are wrong and the shoe will never feel right.
- Qualify **two** cupsole suppliers, not one. Sole supply is the single point of
  failure in sneaker production.
- Decide vulcanised vs. cupsole. Cupsole reads more premium and resoles better.

**Phase 2 — Pattern and prototype (4–6 weeks)**
- Bring the Italian design partner in here. A minimal leather sneaker lives or
  dies on the panel break and the collar line.
- Target as few panels as possible. Every seam is a place to look cheap.
- 3 prototype rounds minimum.

**Phase 3 — Wear test (6–8 weeks, runs in parallel)**
- 10–15 pairs on real feet for 30 days.
- Watch for: heel slip, collar collapse, toe creasing, sole separation.
- **Do not skip this.** It is the difference between a sneaker and an expensive
  disappointment, and it is the step every rushed brand cuts.

**Phase 4 — Pilot run (3–4 weeks)**
- 100–150 pairs. Full size run. 100% inspection.

**Realistic total: 5–6 months** from a standing start to sellable stock.

### 3.4 What the sneaker must not be

- Not a logo shoe. You have no logo equity — a large mark reads as fake.
- Not a lifestyle shoe with a dress-shoe last.
- Not white-on-white unless the leather is genuinely good enough. White exposes
  every flaw in a hide, and there is nowhere to hide.

## 4. Launch plan

### Phase A — Foundations (weeks 1–6)
- [ ] Trademark filed
- [ ] Entity, bank, payment gateway
- [ ] Domain + handle secured, matching
- [ ] Construction claims confirmed in writing
- [ ] Price ladder set against modelled landed cost

### Phase B — Product (weeks 4–12, overlapping)
- [ ] Final 3–4 SKUs confirmed with colourways
- [ ] Retail packaging sourced and sampled
- [ ] **Product photography** — the highest-leverage item on this page
- [ ] 100% QC protocol written
- [ ] Buffer stock built (30–50 pairs/style, core sizes)

### Phase C — Go to market (weeks 10–16)
- [ ] Site live: real prices, real photography, checkout wired
- [ ] Waitlist webhook connected, indexing switched on
- [ ] Instagram: 12–15 posts banked before the account goes public
- [ ] Soft launch to waitlist first — first 50 pairs at a founder price, in
      exchange for a review and a photograph
- [ ] Shipping, returns and repair policies published

### Phase D — After the first hundred pairs
- [ ] Read the returns. Size complaints mean the last is wrong; that is fixable
      and worth fixing immediately.
- [ ] Decide whether the sneaker earns its development slot.
- [ ] Consider one wholesale door in a target market — a single good stockist
      does more for credibility than any ad.

## 5. Numbers to model before launch

Not estimates to guess — the actual figures to sit down and calculate.

| Line | Why |
|---|---|
| **Ex-works cost per pair, by style** | Everything derives from this |
| **Landed cost DDP**, per target market | You are promising duties included |
| **Packaging per unit** | Easy to underestimate by 3× |
| **Payment fees** (2–3%) | |
| **Return rate assumption** (start at 20% for footwear) | The number most first-time DTC brands get catastrophically wrong |
| **Cost of a return** | Shipping both ways + inspection + possible write-down |
| **Contribution margin per pair** | Must survive a return rate of 25% |
| **Customer acquisition cost** | Unknowable until you spend; assume it is worse than you hope |

**The rule of thumb:** at this price point, a DTC brand needs roughly a **65–70%
gross margin** to survive returns and acquisition. Your vertical integration
should make that achievable where a competitor buying finished shoes cannot —
that is the advantage. Do not price it away.

## 6. The three things that will actually decide this

Stripped of everything else:

1. **Photography.** You are selling an object people cannot touch. Every other
   thing on this list matters less. You own a factory and a showroom full of
   finished shoes — you can shoot this week, and it will beat any illustration.
2. **Size accuracy.** Footwear lives and dies on fit. Get the size chart right,
   over-communicate it, and exchange rather than refund.
3. **The story, told once, told well.** "Made by the people who make it" is a
   position almost nobody else can take. Do not dilute it by also claiming to
   be luxury, sustainable, disruptive and timeless.
